I have always found a deep satisfaction in fixing things. As a young kid, I was really talented at taking everything apart. It was an important passtime for me. Putting things back together came a bit later.

I love demystifying things and trying to understand how they work. I also love taking something that's junk and bringing new life to it. Finding an old tool that isn't working and restoring it to functionality brings me great joy, and is also really practical. A lot of these old tools just need a good cleaning and some grease!

## Makita Sander

I needed a sander for the black locust deck you can see on my portfolio. I could've just picked up an M18 battery powered sander for a couple hundred bucks. But I saw an old Makita sander on Facebook Marketplace for $20. 

![Sander](/images/posts/tool_repair/sander.jpg)

They said the backer pad was falling apart and the cable had a bit of minor damage, but that it ran. I found a replacement pad for $15 and figured it'd still be a lot cheaper than a new sander. 

Turns out there was actually a safety recall for this pad--as it breaks down over time, it becomes brittle and chunks start to fly off of it if you try to run it! I took the pad off and found that, unfortunately, this exact model was only briefly produced before they changed the way the pad mounts onto the tool, and so the part I'd found would not be compatible, and in fact I could not find a replacement anywhere. 

![](/images/posts/tool_repair/sander_open.jpg)

After some research, I decided to try scraping the decrepid foam off of the pad and re-using the metal piece that mounted onto the tool. This metal piece had a bearing that was held in place by a plate with four screws. This bearing is what gave the sander it's orbital motion. I wanted to try and get a new pad and affix it to the metal piece, essentially using it as an adapter. I considered using glue or epoxy, but with no way to replace this part I wanted to be able to disassemble it if needed. 

I got a cheap Ryobi backer pad with four mounting screws just to put them next to each other and see if I had any brilliant ideas. I was going to get a thread tapper to create mounting holes in the metal piece, but then I realized an incredible coincidence: the four screws that held the bearing in place somehow lined up perfectly with the four holes on the Ryobi backer! The holes were however on the back of the piece, rather than the front. 

So, I ended up being able to drill out the front of the plate to open up into these screw holes, replace the original screws that held the bearing in place with some shorter screws I got at a hardware store, and thread the new backing plate directly into these mounting holes from the other side.

Sometimes life just works out like that!

Once I got the sander running, I found that it did not have the random orbital motion I'd expect. I opened it back up, cleaned out any dust, and replaced the bearing in the backer pad. It now runs like a dream, and for under $40 I have a really solid Makita sander with a lot of power and excellent dust collection that hopefully will last me for decades!

# Craftsman Band Saw

At a local reuse center, which sells second hand furniture, appliances, electronics, building materials, tools, etc. I found a classic old Craftsman Band Saw for $20. 

![](/images/posts/tool_repair/saw_before.jpg)

It was really dirty and a bit rusty, but overall seemed to be in good shape. I plugged it in to see if it ran, and it made a pretty horrible sound but did run. I made the bet that the sound was the bearings in the wheel guides rather than the motor being shot, and brought it home to open it up!

![](/images/posts/tool_repair/saw_open.jpg)

I sprayed some bike lube on the driven wheel tensioner (the black plastic wheel in the bottom right of the above image that is partly obstructed) and it quieted up immediately! This is not a long term solution, but pointed me to the problem.

I decided to fully take the band saw apart, clean everything up as best I could, and see about re-greasing and bearings I could get to.

![](/images/posts/tool_repair/saw_pieces.jpg)

I also used vinegar to strip rust off of all of the screws and componenets in the bottom and top wheel guide, and the blade tension adjustment, to allow easier and more fine-tuned adjustment of the blade. 

Luckily, the bearings on the wheel tensioners were unsealed, which meant I could easily pop them open, clean out the old crusty grease, and put some fresh grease in there.

![](/images/posts/tool_repair/saw_finished.jpg)

Once I got it all back together and tested it, it sounded so much better! These old tools are really fun to work on. They are sturdy and simple, so repairs are generally pretty doable. It can be a challenge to source parts sometimes, but generally compatible parts can be found or manufactured.

To be honest, this is not exactly the band saw I dream of. I'd love a standing band saw that can support a wider blade with a much larger capacity for resawing, but this is an excellent little saw. It has a really wide throat and is perfect for cutting unique shapes out of plywood, and for cutting small pieces, for example to rough out a general shape for hand carving. And for $20 and an evening of fun, I couldn't pass on it!
