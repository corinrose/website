# Black Locust Deck, South Burlington Vermont

![Deck](/images/build/slocum/slocum2.jpg)

I demoed and re-built a 16' x 50' deck with wraparound stairs. It was a collaborative process--the client did the majority of the design work, including picking out the beautiful, long lasting, and sustainable Black Locust decking. Originally the job was just to replace a few rotten deck boards, but upon investigation I found significant rot on the framing and serious structural issues with the small bumpout seen in the image above.

{{< video src="/videos/bumpout.mp4" >}}

We hired a structural engineer to develop plans for the deck that included tying the improperly supported bumpout into the new deck framing. The client wanted the option of demoing the bumpout in the future and building an addition to their home that extends out further, and so the deck framing was engineered to support that possible future addition. I completed takeoffs based on the engineer's specs and ordered framing and decking materials. We brought in GoliathTech to install the helical pier foundation and do some landscaping work and I completed the structural repair and built the rest of the deck entirely on my own.

![Deck](/images/build/slocum/progress.jpg)

This was my largest solo build to date! The wraparound stairs in particular were a challenge--the deck we were replacing had box framed wraparound stairs, which are much easier to build but are fully enclosed and trap moisture under the deck, which was a big contributor to the rot. So we wanted this new deck to stay open and have lots of air flow.

![Deck](/images/build/slocum/stairs.jpg)

# Savu Sauna, Burlington Vermont

![Saunas](/images/build/savu/double_sauna1.jpg)

In collaboration with two friends, I built saunas for the Burlington, Vermont based [Savu Sauna](https://www.savu.cc/). Initially focusing on optimizing the build process to improve the longevity and function of the saunas, we then worked with Savu to make significant improvements to the design. As the only member of the team who had previous shop experience, I took the lead on managing and developing the shop space. This included building better material storage, installing a dust collection system, building a white board on wheels and some tool carts, maintaining tools, and ensuring safety.


![Saunas](/images/build/savu/tracksaw.jpg)

![Saunas](/images/build/savu/double_sauna.jpg)

The talented and creative owners of Savu have a very clear and stunning aesthetic vision. We were able to work with them to vastly improve the efficiency, sustainability, and ease of building of their saunas while maintaining their aesthetics. This was an excellent opportunity for me to gain a deeper understanding of the entire build process from frame to finish, learn more about building science, and to really hone my finish skills. You can see some of this work on the cedar interior of our first sauna that we built in the mountains of Georgia.

![Saunas](/images/build/savu/georgia_int.jpg)

We were able to work with some absolutely stunning materials, such as Nakamoto's [shou sugi ban collection](https://nakamotoforestry.com/products/shou-sugi-ban/). You can see both Pika-Pika and Gendai siding on the exterior of the same sauna in Georgia.

![Saunas](/images/build/savu/georgia_front.jpg)

Much of our work was building saunas in the shop as orders came in, iterating on the same model with variations in siding, and optimizing our process. We did have some other jobs as well, and we were deeply involved in the design process for the saunas currently operating at [Madbush Falls](https://www.madbushfalls.com/) in Waitsfield, Vermont. These saunas are owned by Savu and booked out by the hour. Some of my notable design contributions are advocating for continuous exterior insulation which vastly increases the energy efficiency and reduces the operating costs of these saunas, designing from scratch a floor system that included a linear drain to allow people to steam the saunas if they choose, and redesigning the interior window trim to allow the cedar to expand and contract freely with the temperature and humidity changes while keeping any gaps hidden.

![Saunas](/images/build/savu/madbush_int.jpg)

# Skin on Frame Canoe, Waitsfield Vermont

![Canoe](/images/build/canoe.jpg)

A friend and I attended Yestermorrow's canoe building class, where we learned the traditional method of building a skin-on-frame canoe. This is in many ways more similar to basket weaving than the woodworking and carpentry I've done professionally. We utilized a lot of steam bending which I was excited to learn about. We then skinned the canoe using fiberglass and a few layers of polyurethane, in place of the traditional animal hide.

![Canoe](/images/build/canoe_water.jpg)

I've largely learned carpentry by doing it in the field, so I was excited by this opportunity to be in a classroom and learn some skills that were outside of my expertise!

# Structural Repair, Essex Vermont

![Structural repair before](/images/build/structural_repair1.jpg)

While working for a contractor based out of Shelburne, Vermont, I did a mix of carpentry, project management, and business development work. One of my projects was leading a structural repair at a log cabin in Essex, Vermont. We and the clients agreed that we wanted to open up the dining room into the main living space to improve circulation and light. However, when we demoed the wall, we found that the beam supporting the second floor was not continuous, and was split over the three studs seen in the image above near the bottom of the stairs. This unfortunately meant that much of the load from the second floor was getting transferred to the triple floor joist adjacent to the stairs, which was inadequate and beginning to deflect, and also meant that we couldn't open up the room without further work.

The contractor I was working under designed and spec'd out a repair where we replaced this split beam with two LVLs spanning the full length of the opening, so that we could install posts and effectively transfer the load to the main beam running through the center of the house and to the foundation wall, and open up the space in the process. 

Having done a number of smaller structural repairs, I led our team of me and two helpers to jack up the second floor with a temporary beam, cut out the old beam, and install the doubled LVL beam and posts.

# Post and Beam Pavilion, Bremen Long Island Maine

![Pavilion](/images/build/bremen/pavilion1.jpg)

I spent most of a summer camped out on Bremen Long Island off the coast of Maine with two carpenter friends. Our main project was the 20' x 30' pavilion pictured above, and we also built a few tent platforms, an outhouse, and insulated and finished a cabin. This pavilion was built to host the reception of the client's wedding later that summer, and was also engineered to support the weight of the client's truck for future storage. It was also built to host an array of solar panels. 

Island builds, we learned, are a unique challenge. We were lucky to have the help and advice of a local who had experience with island builds, and owned a barge to transport materials to the island and some other heavy equipment we made great use of to build the foundation. We however learned that raising trusses on an island without a crane is difficult. See my [blog post](/posts/island) for the full story!

# One Car Garage, Jonesboro Maine

![Garage roof](/images/build/garage_roof.jpg)

At the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, I built a one car garage for my parents. At this point I had a few years of theatrical carpentry experience, but I had never built anything that needed to last longer than the run of a theatrical performance, or needed to stand up to weather of any kind! So we designed this garage, I put together takeoffs and priced it out, and we ordered the materials. I had a beat up old Makita chop saw and a cheap Skil saw with a guard that was only actually perpendicular to the blade when it was set to full depth. My parents got me a drill and impact driver, and with the help of my brother (who had used power tools before but pretty minimally) we framed and roofed this garage in only a week! 

This was the first project where I was fully in charge of the build process. I certainly made some mistakes, but overall we were all very happy with the result and it is still standing to this day!

# Theatrical Carpentry, Vassar College

![The Drama scene shop](/images/build/vassar/shop.jpg)

I worked in the Vassar Drama Department through all four years of my tenure, senior year serving both as the Assistant Technical Director for the department and as the Teaching Assistant for two sections of Drama 103, Intro to Stagecraft. In this time, I built sets, props, and implemented special effects. I gained a lot of experience using power tools, and learned a broad range of skills from painting flats to upholstering furniture. I worked closely with set designers, directors, and the rest of the production team to bring their creative vision to life. 

As the years progressed, I took a more active role in managing the shop, ensuring safe operation of tools, and managing build teams, especially when we had multiple sets being built across the different theatres. The brilliant and creative Paul O'Connor, Technical Director for the Vassar Drama Department, taught me a great deal about carpentry, shop work, managing a team, and most of all bringing an infectious positivity to the job that brings everyone together. 

Paul always had a trick for how to make something look worn in just the right way, or how to get something done faster than you'd think possible and ready for rehearsal later that night. He also went out of his way to teach me skills that I still utilize to this day--like taking the extra time to frame out structures using the same methods we do for building homes, so that I would get even more out of the experience than I already was.

I was also immensely spoiled working in such a clean, organized, well-outfitted shop. I never had to think about ordering materials or build anything that could stand up to weather. We made an active effort to build our set pieces so that we could break them down and reuse them in future productions, but our sets only had to last the length of the production and were only seen from the audience's perspective 20+ feet away, from a few particular angles. When I eventually transitioned into doing exterior work in the field, a lot of my skills transferred, but I had a whole new category of skills to master!
