# Vassar IgniteCS

At Vassar College, my passion for teaching grew, as well as my understanding of the ongoing issues with access to education. This became hard to ignore at Vassar, where the campus itself was this bubble of gorgeous architecture and perfectly manicured flower beds, surrounded by the struggling working class city of Poughkeepsie, New York. There was a street as you leave campus with a bunch of shops and resturaunts clearly marketing entirely to the college students. It was pristine and expensive. As soon as you walked beyond this street, the contrast was stark. In many ways it was a vibrant community, but with shuttered businesses and dilapidated homes dotting the streets at the last stop on New York City's Metro-North line. 

Working under the existing computer science club, a small group of me and maybe two or three other students  reached out to local middle schools and developed a program to provide weekly after school classes covering fun and practical computer science skills. We acquired funding through Google's IgniteCS program, and our first year taught students to use Arduinos, open source microcontrollers that we hoped would show the students a bit of hardware and software, and excite them enough to continue pursuing their education.

Our second year, we doubled the number of Vassar students involved and narrowed our focus to teaching Python programming. This allowed us to dig into greater depth, rather than splitting focus between learning the hardware and programming. It also allowed us to break the course up and have different Vassar students work on different lectures, so we could handle a significantly larger cohort of students and range of topics. By eliminating our hardware costs, we were also able to put our limited budget towards providing food which we hoped would encourage lower income students who otherwise would be more likely to miss out on these kind of opportunities to consider joining. It was incredibly satisfying seeing some of the students from our first year return! 

You can read [this article](https://www.vassar.edu/stories/2017/171207-teaching-kids-to-code.html) about this year's program.

The third year, we were very excited to have much of the groundwork already done. We had lectures written, we had relationships with local middle schools, and we even had repeat students who were encouraging their friends and younger students to join! Unfortunately, Google's IgniteCS program completely restructured without much warning. They would no longer provide funding, and the optional coursework they'd provided was now required, with no flexibility to teach what we wanted. This coursework was entirely pen and paper, and while that is absolutely a legitimate way of teaching computer science, we felt that the students we were working with would not be excited by a course that didn't actually use computers at all. More importantly, we felt that with our particular goal of encouraging young people who otherwise might not think that computer science is welcoming for them, getting these students comfortable actually using a computer to solve problems was paramount. Our biggest goal was to get these students passionte enough about what they started with us to continue. So, we ended our relationship with IgniteCS, and with the success of our previous year, were able to secure funding through Vassar's Student Government to continue providing food.

This year, we took our existing lectures on the fundementals of Python programming, and added a series of lectures on broader topics in computer science and technology that we hoped would be exiting and interesting to these students.

# Vassar CS Summer Camp

Following the success of these courses, Jason Waterman, my favorite CS professor at Vassar, reached out to me and my friend Ian who were the principle content writers for the program about teaching a more more extensive summer CS camp aimed in particular at Vassar students who had no background in computer science. This course was loosely based on Harvey Mudd's intoduction to comptuer science as well as our lecture material, with the hope being to eventually replace Vassar's existing CMPU101 introductory class, which taught Scheme and was overly focused on theory and not up to date with modern programming techniques. 

This course was six weeks long, five days a week. The first four weeks were structured with a lecture from Prof. Waterman in the morning, and then after lunch a shorter lecture from Ian and I with a project we had prepared that the students could work on with our help. The final two weeks, we helped the students develop ideas for projects and implement them.

The content we wrote for this course can be found on this [GitHub repository](https://github.com/corinrose/vassar-cscamp-website/blob/master/website/content/_index.md).

# Math Education, Kent's Hill School

After college, I worked at Kent's Hill School in Maine as a part time math teacher, filling in for a teacher who had unexpectedly left. I taught a section of Honors Geometry and a much larger class of Algebra 1. The head of the math department was the only full time member of the math faculty, and she was brilliant but severely over-leveraged. Once she saw that I was competent, I was more or less fully left on my own to develop lectures, homeworks, and tests and run the classes as I chose. This was an exciting opportunity, but also very daunting to be thrown into the deep end by myself! For the Honors Geometry class, I was provided a textbook that was really useful and well thought out, so this gave me a really good basis to go off of, but still had to develop lecture slides, homeworks, and tests. For the Algebra class, the textbook I was provided was, in my opinion, really poorly thought out. The class was also significantly larger and more differentiated, with freshman, softmores, and even one junior of varying ability and interest, and a group of students for whom English was their second language. 

One of these student's had excellent English. He clearly wanted to be a bit of a trouble maker. But I made it impossible for him! He would often ask questions that he thought would stump or frustrate me, like asking why we are doing something a particular way or why we are even learning something. He did not anticipate that I would genuinely love these questions, and give him real answers. Sometimes, that answer would be that this particular thing we are learning is actually useless, but they will be expected to know it in their future classes. But more often than not, I'd have the context to actually provide him a satisfying answer, and regardless he clearly appreciated my candor. We ended up developing a great relationship, he would genuinely get excited when he started to understand a concept which was so satisfying to see. I relied on him heavily to help me explain ideas to the other Spainish speaker's whose English was not as good.

Kent's Hill School offered me a full time position and to fund my Master's in Education at the University of Maine in Farmington, but I'd been contacted about doing the carpentry work on Bremen Long Island referenced on my build portfolio, which was too interesting of a project to turn down!
