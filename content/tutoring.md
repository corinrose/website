## How I Approach Teaching

Most STEM anxiety comes from treating math and physics like a list of arbitrary rules to memorize. This prepares students for the test, but it does not give them any actual problem solving skills for when they come across new problems in future classes, or for when they run into actual problems in the real world. 

My approach is different. I break down complex theoretical ideas into clear, intuitive mental models. Having spent my career moving between abstract computer science, formal mathematics, startup engineering, and physical carpentry, I help students bridge that gap by getting to know them as a person, and grounding abstract mathematics with concrete examples drawn from their interests.

When a student connects an abstract formula with a real concept they have an intuitive grasp of, they start to understand *why* a formula works, which allows them to generalize that understanding to more abstract contexts.

I also show students the process. A lot of students who struggle with STEM see a problem and feel lost about how to even approach finding a solution, and so they give up. They're taught that they have a list of equations and techniques to solve problems, and that's all math is: matching up problem solving techniques from your list to the problem and plugging some numbers in.

This misses that problem solving is a creative process. When a talented math student meets a totally new problem, they feel the same confusion and discomfort as everyone else. The difference is that they try something anyway, even if it feels silly: write down whatever comes to mind and see if seeing it on paper sparks something. Maybe it won’t, but there’s no way to know until you try. And nearly all math feels simple and obvious once you get it. Even 1 + 1 = 2 took Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead hundreds of pages of groundwork in *Principia Mathematica* before they reached a proof. Part of my job is helping students sit with that discomfort and see that it isn’t a sign they’re bad at math. It’s the process everyone goes through, even the most talented mathematicians, whenever they encounter something new.
